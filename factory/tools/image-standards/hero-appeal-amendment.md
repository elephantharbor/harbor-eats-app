# FlavorWeave Meal Image Specification
## Amendment — Hero Food Photography & Commercial Appeal Standard

### Status

This amendment extends the existing **FlavorWeave Meal Image Specification** and the Catalog Factory image-certification requirements.

It does not replace the existing requirements for:

- recipe fidelity,
- accurate ingredients,
- AI-only image generation,
- known provenance,
- required WebP assets,
- crop safety,
- active/rejected asset handling,
- and deterministic Freeze Integrity.

Where this amendment is more specific about visual quality, use this amendment.

---

# 1. Purpose

FlavorWeave meal imagery is not merely recipe documentation.

It is a primary consumer-product surface.

Meal images influence:

- whether a recommendation looks desirable,
- whether several choices feel meaningfully different,
- perceived product quality,
- trust in the recipe,
- and whether FlavorWeave feels like a premium consumer food product.

Accordingly, every published FlavorWeave meal image must satisfy two independent standards:

## A. Recipe Fidelity

> **Does this image truthfully depict the meal FlavorWeave is offering?**

and:

## B. Hero Appeal

> **Does this image make the real meal look genuinely desirable in a premium consumer food application?**

An image must pass **both**.

Accuracy does not excuse dull imagery.

Beauty does not excuse inaccurate imagery.

---

# 2. North-star visual reference

The established FlavorWeave catalog contains successful imagery exemplified by the supplied **Chipotle Lime Black Bean Bowl** reference.

Use that image as a **house-style reference**, not as a literal composition template.

Important qualities demonstrated by the reference include:

- bright, soft, natural-looking light,
- vivid but believable food color,
- strong separation between components,
- abundant presentation without looking chaotic,
- food occupying most of the useful frame,
- fresh finishing elements,
- visible textural contrast,
- appetizing char and surface detail,
- clean light-neutral dishware,
- restrained neutral background,
- subtle environmental ingredients around the hero dish,
- premium editorial-food-photography character,
- and strong visual performance when reduced to a FlavorWeave recommendation card.

Do not make every meal:

- a bowl,
- overhead,
- highly colorful,
- cilantro-garnished,
- lime-garnished,
- or composed identically.

The objective is to reproduce the **quality, appetite appeal, light, intentionality, and product-family consistency**, not the exact staging.

---

# 3. Default creative direction

Unless the meal itself requires a different treatment, newly generated FlavorWeave images should target:

> **Bright, premium, realistic editorial food photography designed for a modern consumer meal-planning application.**

The result should feel closer to professionally art-directed recipe photography than:

- a clinical catalog photograph,
- a generic AI food render,
- a restaurant menu thumbnail,
- or a plain QA reference image.

The food should look:

- freshly prepared,
- appetizing,
- abundant enough to feel satisfying,
- texturally interesting,
- realistically plated,
- and ready to eat.

---

# 4. Craveability is a publication requirement

A technically accurate image may still fail publication.

Before certification, ask:

> **Would this image make a reasonable consumer more interested in choosing this meal?**

The answer must be yes.

Common characteristics of an image that should be challenged or regenerated include:

- flat or lifeless lighting,
- beige-on-beige presentation with no visual relief where relief is realistically possible,
- food centered mechanically with excessive unused plate space,
- weak textural detail,
- dry-looking meat/fish/grains,
- pale food that should have visible browning or char,
- sparse plating inconsistent with the actual serving,
- lack of visual focal point,
- ingredients blending into one undifferentiated mass,
- unintentional sterile appearance,
- or presentation that looks more like recipe evidence than consumer-facing hero photography.

Do not certify a mediocre image merely because nothing is technically wrong with it.

---

# 5. Lighting standard

Prefer:

- bright diffused natural-window-light character,
- soft directional illumination,
- realistic highlights,
- gentle natural shadows,
- enough dimensionality to reveal texture.

Food should appear illuminated and fresh.

Avoid by default:

- harsh flash,
- dramatic restaurant-dark lighting,
- muddy shadows,
- blown highlights,
- flat shadowless rendering,
- heavy yellow cast,
- gray/desaturated treatment,
- excessive HDR appearance,
- obviously synthetic glossy lighting.

The visual system is primarily light, warm, fresh, and food-forward.

---

# 6. Color standard

Images should use the natural colors of the actual recipe to create visual energy.

Favor:

- distinct ingredient colors,
- fresh greens where legitimately present,
- browning/char where the cooking method creates it,
- sauces that retain believable richness,
- colorful vegetables/components when part of the recipe,
- natural contrast between food elements.

Color should be:

> **vibrant but believable**

not:

> artificially oversaturated.

Do not alter the implied recipe simply to obtain more color.

If a meal is naturally monochromatic, improve the photograph through:

- lighting,
- texture,
- plating,
- composition,
- vessel choice,
- legitimate finishing ingredients,
- sauce presentation,
- and background context

rather than inventing colorful ingredients.

---

# 7. Texture visibility

The image should visually communicate the recipe's important textures.

Examples:

If the meal is described as:

- crispy → show believable browned/crisp surfaces,
- grilled → show appropriate grill marks/char,
- creamy → show the actual creamy component,
- glazed → show believable glaze,
- crunchy → make the relevant component visibly distinct,
- tender → avoid visually dry or tough protein,
- roasted → show appropriate caramelization.

Consumer-facing claims must be visually supported where reasonably visible.

---

# 8. Composition

The hero meal must remain the unmistakable focal point.

Prefer:

- intentional composition,
- food occupying a substantial portion of the frame,
- enough negative space to breathe without appearing sparse,
- clear separation of important components,
- layered visual depth,
- crop-safe placement.

Avoid:

- tiny food surrounded by plate,
- unnecessary wide tabletop views,
- perfectly rigid symmetry unless appropriate,
- clutter competing with the dish,
- centered-documentation-shot appearance on every meal.

Composition should feel deliberately art-directed.

---

# 9. Camera angle

Choose the angle that best communicates the actual meal.

Potential approaches include:

## Slightly elevated 3/4 angle

Strong default for:

- plated entrées,
- sandwiches,
- steaks,
- fish,
- composed plates,
- casseroles.

Provides dimensionality and texture.

## Higher 3/4 / near-overhead angle

Strong for:

- bowls,
- ingredient-rich plates,
- curries,
- pasta,
- composed grain dishes.

## Lower angle

Use selectively when needed to show:

- sandwich layers,
- stacked food,
- meaningful vertical structure.

Do not impose one angle across the entire catalog.

Choose the angle to make the real food most understandable and attractive.

---

# 10. Dishware and background

Default visual environment should remain coherent with FlavorWeave's established imagery.

Prefer:

- modern ceramic dishes,
- off-white / warm-neutral / light stoneware,
- clean simple bowls and plates,
- light neutral tabletop or background,
- subtle natural material texture where appropriate.

Avoid:

- ornate patterned dishes,
- restaurant-logo plates,
- highly saturated dishware competing with food,
- dark nightclub-style surfaces,
- excessive props,
- overly rustic styling unless genuinely appropriate to the dish.

The food remains the visual brand hero.

---

# 11. Garnish policy

Garnish is encouraged when it improves appetite appeal **and is truthful**.

On-plate garnish must be one of:

1. an actual ingredient in the recipe,
2. an explicitly listed finishing ingredient,
3. an explicitly optional garnish represented in recipe metadata.

Do not visually introduce an unlisted ingredient merely because it makes the image prettier.

Examples of useful legitimate finishing elements might include:

- chopped herbs,
- scallions,
- citrus zest,
- citrus wedges,
- sesame,
- chili flakes,
- sauce drizzle,
- pickled vegetables,

**only when appropriate to that recipe.**

Do not create a default "cilantro + lime" visual language for unrelated meals.

---

# 12. Environmental ingredient styling

The hero dish may be accompanied by a small number of contextual food ingredients around the plate, similar to the established reference image.

Examples:

- a lime half,
- a sprig/bunch of an herb,
- a relevant chile,
- a small scattering of an actual recipe ingredient.

These elements can create:

- color,
- depth,
- freshness,
- editorial polish.

Rules:

- environmental ingredients should come from or directly correspond to the recipe,
- they must not imply an additional served side,
- they must not dominate the composition,
- they must not compromise crop safety,
- they must not become repetitive visual clichés across the catalog.

Props should support the meal, not decorate around a weak hero.

---

# 13. Sauce and finish presentation

Where the recipe contains an important sauce, dressing, relish, glaze, broth, or condiment:

- represent it visibly where appropriate,
- distribute it naturally,
- make it look freshly prepared,
- ensure its amount is plausible relative to the recipe.

Do not depict:

- a sauce absent from the recipe,
- excessive glaze purely for shine,
- restaurant-style drizzles not produced by the instructions,
- or a condiment that would materially change the eating experience.

---

# 14. Protein presentation

For meat and fish, imagery must make doneness and preparation believable.

Examples:

## Steak

Should reflect:

- specified cut,
- actual slicing/whole presentation,
- plausible requested doneness,
- actual grill/sear treatment.

## Fish

Should reflect:

- correct species/form factor where visually relevant,
- cooking method,
- thickness,
- browning/char.

## Chicken/pork

Should not look:

- raw,
- unnaturally uniform,
- dry,
- deep-fried when pan-seared,
- heavily breaded when lightly coated.

Use appetizing moisture and surface texture without unrealistic shine.

---

# 15. Plant-forward meals require equal hero treatment

Do not visually downgrade meals merely because they are plant-based.

Plant-forward imagery should be as:

- abundant,
- intentional,
- colorful where appropriate,
- textural,
- polished,
- desirable

as meat/fish imagery.

Avoid the visual implication:

> "This is the healthy/plain option."

FlavorWeave should make plant-forward meals look like first-choice dinners.

---

# 16. Plating brief is required before image generation

Before generating the image, Juniper must create a short structured **Image / Plating Brief**.

At minimum:

### Hero subject

What is visually dominant?

### Vessel

Plate, shallow bowl, deep bowl, sandwich plate, skillet presentation, etc.

### Camera angle

Chosen deliberately.

### Required visible components

What must be visible for recipe fidelity?

### Texture cues

What texture must photography communicate?

### Sauce/component placement

How should important sauces/components appear?

### Legitimate garnish

Which recipe-listed finishing elements may be used?

### Environmental styling

Which, if any, actual recipe ingredients may appear around the plate?

### Color opportunities

Which real ingredients provide natural visual contrast?

### Forbidden elements

Anything that the generator must not introduce.

### Crop strategy

How should the main subject remain effective at:

- 1200×900 master,
- 640×480/card derivative,
- current FlavorWeave card crop.

This brief becomes part of the image-generation artifact.

---

# 17. Image-generation prompt should contain both truth and art direction

The generation instruction should derive from:

- exact recipe,
- Plating Brief,
- FlavorWeave house style.

Do not generate from the meal title alone.

The prompt should communicate:

1. what the dish actually contains,
2. how it is actually cooked,
3. what it must visibly show,
4. how it should be plated,
5. how it should be lit/composed,
6. what must not appear.

This reduces attractive-but-inaccurate generation.

---

# 18. Hero Appeal certification sub-gate

Extend image certification so image review explicitly contains two independent decisions.

## K — Image Fidelity

Existing requirement.

PASS only when the image accurately represents the recipe.

## L1 — Image Technical / Style Compliance

Existing technical/style requirements:

- file format,
- dimensions,
- crop safety,
- house style,
- forbidden artifacts,
- provenance,
- etc.

## L2 — Hero Appeal / Craveability

New mandatory sub-gate.

PASS only when Vale determines that the image meets the premium consumer-product quality bar.

Vale should explicitly evaluate:

- appetite appeal,
- lighting,
- color,
- texture,
- composition,
- plating,
- focal clarity,
- visual richness,
- premium feel,
- card-scale effectiveness.

A technically compliant but visually mediocre image receives:

`L2 FAIL`

and must be regenerated or restyled.

There is no `PASS WITH NOTES` publication state.

---

# 19. Hero Appeal questions for Vale

Vale should answer, internally or in structured audit form:

1. Does this look genuinely appetizing?
2. Does the food look freshly prepared?
3. Is the hero ingredient immediately understandable?
4. Is there useful visual contrast?
5. Are important textures visible?
6. Does the lighting flatter the food without looking artificial?
7. Does the plating feel intentional?
8. Does the frame feel abundant rather than sparse?
9. Would this image remain attractive as a small recommendation card?
10. Does this feel visually consistent with FlavorWeave's strongest existing meal imagery?
11. Is it attractive **without violating recipe fidelity?**

If a material answer is no, L2 should ordinarily fail.

---

# 20. Comparison against reference quality

For initial Catalog Factory runs and Legacy Audit, Vale should conceptually compare candidate imagery against the supplied established FlavorWeave reference quality.

Ask:

> If this new image appeared next to the strongest existing FlavorWeave cards, would it feel equally premium and desirable?

If obviously not:

`FAIL`

This is a relative product-quality check, not a requirement for identical composition.

---

# 21. Regeneration should target the actual weakness

When an image fails, record why.

Examples:

- fidelity problem,
- weak color separation,
- flat lighting,
- too sparse,
- wrong texture,
- wrong ingredient,
- incorrect coating,
- poor crop,
- overly clinical presentation,
- insufficient visual abundance.

The next generation should address the diagnosed problem rather than rerolling blindly.

Retain rejected assets according to existing rejected-artifact rules.

---

# 22. Avoid artificial food styling

Premium presentation must remain credible.

Prohibit:

- excessive fake gloss,
- impossible char,
- unrealistic ingredient abundance,
- giant portions unsupported by recipe,
- duplicate garnishes appearing unnaturally,
- gravity-defying stacks,
- excessive steam,
- impossible melting/sauce behavior,
- visual components whose only purpose is decoration.

The target is:

> **professionally styled real food**

not:

> **advertising fantasy food.**

---

# 23. Avoid visual sameness across the catalog

House style should create coherence, not cloning.

Do not produce 100 images that all have:

- identical white bowl,
- identical 45° angle,
- identical parsley/cilantro garnish,
- identical citrus wedge,
- identical shadows,
- identical background props.

Allow the dish to determine:

- plate/bowl,
- angle,
- composition,
- garnish,
- context.

The family resemblance should come from:

- lighting quality,
- realism,
- polish,
- neutral presentation environment,
- intentional styling,
- and premium food-first photography.

---

# 24. Card-scale test

Every candidate image should be inspected at approximately the actual recommendation-card presentation size.

An image may look strong full-size but weak when reduced.

PASS requires:

- focal meal remains immediately identifiable,
- key components remain legible,
- crop does not remove defining parts,
- colors remain distinct,
- composition does not become visually muddy.

This should be part of L2 / Hero Appeal.

---

# 25. Legacy 24 implication

When the existing 24 meals undergo Legacy Audit and image replacement:

Do not merely regenerate mechanically accurate replicas.

The replacement program should use this upgraded Hero Photography standard.

The desired outcome is:

> **retain or improve the visual quality of the strongest legacy imagery while gaining clean AI provenance and recipe fidelity.**

A new AI image should not replace an attractive existing image with a noticeably duller one merely because rights/provenance are cleaner.

Replacement must preserve or raise the visual bar.

---

# 26. Core image principle

The Catalog Factory should permanently operate under:

> **Truthful enough to trust. Attractive enough to choose.**

And:

> **Make the real meal more craveable — never make the image less truthful.**

Both are required for publication.
